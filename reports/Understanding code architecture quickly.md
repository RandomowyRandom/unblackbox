# Chunk it, trace it, explain why

People understand a complex architecture fastest when an explanation does three things that reading the code cannot. It hands them **a few named, pre-built chunks, about four per view, which is the core limit of working memory**. It **runs one concrete request or event through those chunks**, so the resulting mental model predicts behaviour instead of merely listing parts. And it **states why the design is the way it is**, the knowledge newcomers consistently report as hardest to recover. Durability needs a fourth step the explainer cannot do alone: the learner has to retrieve, predict or explore, because passive viewing is the weakest form of engagement and reliably produces more confidence than competence. The mechanisms behind each step are well supported. Independent meta-analyses put signaling, segmenting, case comparison, problem-first sequencing and retrieval practice in a band of roughly g = 0.3 to 0.6, and a controlled experiment found that giving developers a runtime trace visualization cut comprehension time by **22%** and raised correct answers by **43%** ([Cornelissen et al. 2011](https://azaidman.github.io/publications/cornelissenTSE2010.pdf)). But no study has tested short narrated architecture videos directly, so every design rule here is an extrapolation that unblackbox should treat as a hypothesis to measure. For unblackbox, the implication is that the hard problem is selection, not rendering: the agent must choose the four chunks, the one trace, the contrast case and the rationale, then pace a system-paced video so the viewer never has to hold more in mind than the screen shows. Its current primitives (short beats, line focus, token morphing, semantic colours, sidecar captions) already match the best-supported levers. What is missing is a zoom move between abstraction levels, a way to light up and animate a multi-element request path, a predict-and-pause beat, an audience-level setting, and structural guards against the curse of knowledge in the agent that writes the script.

## Working memory holds about four chunks, so the explainer must supply them

The binding constraint on fast understanding is working memory, and it is tighter than folklore suggests. When rehearsal and recoding are blocked, the core short-term capacity limit is **about four chunks, not Miller's seven** ([Cowan 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11515286/)). Cognitive load theory adds that difficulty depends on *element interactivity*, meaning how many elements must be held at once to make sense of any one of them. A software architecture sits near the maximum. A request path through controller, service, repository, cache and queue cannot be understood one box at a time. For such high-interactivity material, studying worked examples beats generating solutions; generation only wins for low-interactivity material ([Chen, Kalyuga & Sweller 2015](https://repository.lboro.ac.uk/articles/journal_contribution/The_worked_example_effect_the_generation_effect_and_element_interactivity/12053721)). People get past the four-chunk ceiling by holding bigger chunks, not more of them: a schema stored in long-term memory enters working memory as a single element ([Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas 2019](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/cognitive-architecture-and-instructional-design-20years-later/)).

That is exactly what programming expertise looks like. Expert programmers organize programming concepts **by function, while beginners organize them by everyday-language associations** ([McKeithen et al. 1981](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/items/1d548813-cc8c-4d00-bc3b-ef1cbd11ed0d)). Experts also rely on "programming plans", which are stereotyped solution fragments, and on conventions of discourse. Their advantage **shrank on "unplan-like" programs** that broke those conventions ([Soloway & Ehrlich 1984](https://research.cs.queensu.ca/home/cordy/cisc860/Biblio/hurd/misc/soloway84.pdf)). Expertise is a library of named shapes, and it pays off only when the code matches a known shape. Neuroimaging points the same way. Comprehension guided by semantic cues needed less brain activation in all measured areas than bottom-up decoding ([Siegmund et al. 2017](https://www.se.cs.uni-saarland.de/publications/docs/SPP+17.pdf)). In a 19-participant fMRI study, more than 41 complexity metrics explained cognitive effort only weakly. Textual size drove attention, and **vocabulary size, meaning the number of distinct identifiers and operators, burdened working memory** ([Peitek et al. 2021](https://archive.cps-vo.org/node/81592)). A larger study found that **none of 121 metrics** correlated significantly with perceived or actual understandability across 444 evaluations ([Scalabrino et al. 2019](https://www.cs.wm.edu/~denys/pubs/TSE%2719-Understandability.pdf)). Understandability cannot be read off the code with a metric. It has to be designed into the explanation.

Instruction can hand learners ready-made chunks directly, and three tested moves do it. *Pre-training* gives learners the names and characteristics of key concepts before the main lesson (Mayer-lab median d = 0.46) ([Mayer 2017](https://synapsesocial.com/papers/W2625051696)). *Subgoal labels* in expository text and worked examples improved novel problem solving in programming, though the two placements were not additive ([Margulieux & Catrambone 2016](https://scholarworks.gsu.edu/items/80aa2cc7-cce3-4d9f-8b7c-c6e21bd3cb58)). And *naming the pattern* works as a pre-built chunk. In two controlled experiments, with 74 German graduate students in Java and 22 US undergraduates in C++ on 360 to 560 line programs, **pattern-relevant maintenance was faster or less error-prone when comments named the design patterns**, even though the names added no new facts ([Prechelt et al. 2002](https://cris.fau.de/publications/115927284)). Names themselves matter too. Single letters and abbreviations slowed comprehension compared with full words ([Hofmeister, Siegmund & Holt 2017](https://www.se.cs.uni-saarland.de/publications/docs/HoSeHo17.pdf)), although three 2017 studies disagreed on the size of the effect ([Never Work in Theory](https://neverworkintheory.org/2021/08/09/abbreviated-vs-full-names.html)).

Two consequences follow. First, a whole system fits inside the four-chunk budget only through hierarchy: four boxes, then zoom into one and show its three or four internals, then zoom back out. Every major architecture-description approach converges on this. C4 calls its diagrams **"maps of your code" at zoom levels, explicitly compared with Google Maps** ([C4 model](https://c4model.com/introduction)), and arc42 refines its building-block view hierarchically ([arc42](https://arc42.org/overview)). Second, the right amount of scaffolding depends on who is watching. The *expertise reversal effect* is the finding that techniques highly effective for novices "can lose their effectiveness and even have negative consequences" for experienced learners ([Kalyuga et al. 2003](https://ro.uow.edu.au/edupapers/136)). It has been shown within a single study, where high guidance helped novices and more knowledgeable learners did better generating answers ([Chen, Kalyuga & Sweller 2016](https://repository.lboro.ac.uk/articles/journal_contribution/Relations_between_the_worked_example_and_generation_effects_on_immediate_and_delayed_tests/12052686)). For an expert, the fastest explanation names the known pattern ("a standard outbox, except...") and spends its time on the deviations. For a novice, the same label is noise until it is unpacked once. Because novices group by surface words, near-synonyms such as "handler" and "controller" need an explicit contrast.

## A mental model must run: one traced request beats a complete diagram

A usable mental model of a system has four parts: the components and their responsibilities, the connections between them (data and control flow), the state (what is stored where, and for how long), and the causal dynamics (what happens over time to one request, including under failure). The test of having such a model is prediction. Multimedia-learning research measures meaningful understanding with transfer questions of the "what would happen if..." kind ([Mayer 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-023-09842-1)). Narration that lists components without running anything through them builds a static picture that cannot answer those questions.

Program-comprehension research supports a clear order: **control flow first, then function and data flow**. In Pennington's study of 80 professional programmers (40 COBOL, 40 FORTRAN), readers of unfamiliar code first built a "program model" of control flow and elementary operations. A "situation model" of goals, functions and data flow in domain terms emerged only with further study ([Pennington 1987](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA179392.pdf)). This is the clearest empirical ordering claim in the field, though it comes from short programs. Littman and colleagues observed two strategies among experienced programmers. **Systematic tracing of global data and control flow built knowledge of causal interactions and was associated with successful modifications. "As-needed" local reading missed those interactions and more often failed** ([Littman et al. 1987](https://typeset.io/papers/mental-models-and-software-maintenance-3mauac5iji)). That is a small protocol study, but it names the classic newcomer error: a change that is locally correct and globally breaking. Eye tracking shows that experts follow execution rather than file order. Experts read code only **about 60% linearly, against about 70% for novices and about 80% for novices reading prose** ([Busjahn et al. 2015](https://huang.isis.vanderbilt.edu/cs8395-f23/paper/linear-order-eye.pdf)). The code's own structure shapes reading order even more strongly than expertise ([Peitek, Siegmund & Apel 2020](https://www.se.cs.uni-saarland.de/publications/docs/PSA20.pdf)), and in realistic IDE sessions developers concentrate on method bodies and call terms ([Abid et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.03358)).

The strongest software-specific number in this research comes from showing developers what the system actually does at runtime. On eight comprehension tasks, developers given the EXTRAVIS trace visualization alongside Eclipse needed **22% less time and gave 43% more correct answers** than an Eclipse-only group ([Cornelissen et al. 2011](https://azaidman.github.io/publications/cornelissenTSE2010.pdf)). The baseline was a bare IDE, so the result shows that a structured runtime view beats none, not that one visual style beats another. Architecture frameworks encode the same insight. Kruchten's 4+1 model adds a "+1" set of scenarios that ties its four structural views together and validates them ([Kruchten 1995](https://www.ics.uci.edu/~michele/Teaching/INF117-S08/Krutchten%204+1View%20SWArch.pdf)). arc42 has a dedicated runtime view ([arc42](https://arc42.org/overview)), and C4 has dynamic diagrams ([C4 model](https://c4model.com/introduction)). Bret Victor makes the general argument: insight comes from moving up and down a ladder of abstraction, and stepping down to the concrete case is "as important as stepping up" ([Victor 2011](http://worrydream.com/LadderOfAbstraction/)).

No single comprehension model wins, which is why the trace needs a frame around it. Storey's synthesis concludes that the diversity of comprehension theories reflects real differences in programs, programmers and tasks, and that developers switch strategies ([Storey 2006](https://www.bibsonomy.org/bibtex/1af82bc48bcfb8197b6767f190ee388bf/sjbutler)). The practical recipe combines the models. State the system's purpose as a top-down hypothesis ("this is an event bus"). Anchor it in one execution path. Then link the levels explicitly so the viewer can climb between box and line. This is also where existing tools are weakest. Sillito and colleagues catalogued 44 kinds of developer questions in four categories, from finding initial focus points up to **understanding relationships among groups of code, and tools supported the narrow questions far better than the broad ones** ([Sillito et al. 2008](https://manuscript.isc.ac/Inventory/49/1142180.htm)). Developers rated on average about 4.1 of 12 reachability questions at least somewhat hard, experience did not make them easier, and one such question often took tens of minutes ([LaToza & Myers 2010](https://memphis-cs.github.io/comp-7085-8085-2012-fall/papers/LaToza2010ICSE.pdf)). Professionals spend **about 58% of their working time on comprehension** ([Xia et al. 2018](https://xin-xia.github.io/publication/TSE17.pdf)). An explanation that answers "how do these parts relate when a request flows through them" targets the most expensive and least-tooled question developers have. A developer exploring a codebase alone should follow the same recipe: form a hypothesis about purpose, find the entry point, trace one request systematically, ask why, then experiment.

## Rationale and contrast are what newcomers cannot recover alone

What a system contains can be recovered from its code with effort. Why it is built that way usually cannot. In a grounded-theory study of 18 newcomers across 18 IBM projects, **low-level design and runtime behaviour were the knowledge most critical to integration and often the least documented. Rationale was recovered by asking people** ([Dagenais et al. 2010](https://www.cs.mcgill.ca/~martin/papers/icse2010.pdf)). Microsoft developers "go to great lengths to create and maintain rich mental models of code", rarely rely on external representations, and keep their models in sync through face-to-face conversation, which slows newcomers ([LaToza, Venolia & DeLine 2006](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=154508)). Whiteboard drawings that documented design decisions were often lost ([Cherubini et al. 2007](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=154511)), and practitioners value rationale but report barriers to recording it ([Tang et al. 2006](https://figshare.swinburne.edu.au/articles/report/A_survey_of_architecture_design_rationale/26269849)). Nygard's case for Architecture Decision Records (ADRs) states the cost plainly: without recorded rationale, a newcomer can only blindly accept a past decision or blindly change it ([Nygard 2011](https://cognitect.com/blog/2011/11/15/documenting-architecture-decisions)). The practice is thin in the wild, though. About half of GitHub repositories that use ADRs hold only one to five of them ([IEEE Access 2023](https://doaj.org/article/f838f2a538ea48e7a3b15ce99befe612)), and evidence for ADRs covers perceived usefulness, not measured comprehension. Diátaxis treats this kind of "why" writing as its own genre, *explanation*. Explanation is understanding-oriented, bounded by a question, and carries design decisions, history, constraints and weighed alternatives ([Diátaxis](https://diataxis.fr/explanation/)).

The order in which ideas arrive matters as much as their content, and the evidence favours **problem before solution**. Across 53 studies and 166 comparisons, solving a problem before instruction beat instruction-first at **g = 0.36** (95% CI 0.20 to 0.51), rising to 0.58 when designs followed productive-failure principles closely. The effect reversed for children in grades 2 to 5 and for domain-general skills ([Sinha & Kapur 2021](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1406031499744456704)). A more conservative meta-analysis found a non-significant g = 0.22 against other sensemaking activities ([ETH Research Collection](https://www.research-collection.ethz.ch/entities/publication/087241d3-ca0e-469e-a7f3-df9c122e2585)), so the honest reading is a modest, real benefit for adults. That benefit depends on full instruction following the struggle. It is not a licence for long unguided puzzles, which cognitive load theory predicts will hurt novices on high-interactivity material.

Comparison is the strongest tool for turning a concrete case into an abstraction. Across 57 experiments and 336 tests, **case comparisons beat traditional instruction at d = 0.50**. Stating the principle *after* the comparison worked better, and asking learners to find only similarities beat asking for similarities and differences ([Alfieri, Nokes-Malach & Schunn 2013](https://www.lrdc.pitt.edu/Schunn/papers/ContrastingCasesMeta-AlfieriEtAl2013.pdf)). Concreteness fading, which moves from concrete instances toward abstract representations, has supportive primary studies ([Fyfe et al. 2014](https://pc.cogs.indiana.edu/?p=885)). It has no pooled effect size, though, and one study found a concrete-only condition matched it on transfer ([Kokkonen & Schalk](https://www.utupub.fi/items/101ac9b4-f708-4def-816a-78827b0fc696/full)). Analogies need even more care. In the classic experiment, people rarely applied an analogous story to a new problem until told it was relevant ([Gick & Holyoak 1980](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285%2880%2990013-4)). Teachers in higher-achieving systems presented source and target together ([Richland, Zur & Holyoak 2007](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1142103)). Both findings come from background summaries in the notes and were not re-verified. The working rule is that "a queue is like a post office" helps only when the relational mapping is shown and the point where the analogy breaks is stated.

Design patterns sharpen all of this. A mapping study of the Gang-of-Four literature found that **patterns do not appear to help novices learn design**, with some qualitative support for maintenance benefits ([Zhang & Budgen 2012](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/output/1476205/what-do-we-know-about-the-effectiveness-of-software-design-patterns)). Educators describe lecture-first pattern teaching as "particularly poorly suited" and advocate problem-based approaches ([Ó Cinnéide & Tynan 2004](https://eprints.teachingandlearning.ie/id/eprint/3312/)). Read together with Prechelt, the pattern name speeds up people who already own the chunk and teaches nothing to people who do not. The sequence that fits the evidence is: show the code without the pattern, make the pain concrete, refactor, and name the pattern last. This happens to be the CodeAesthetic shape, although the problem-first versus solution-first comparison has never been isolated experimentally for patterns. Explaining legacy code calls for selectivity. A single Blob or Spaghetti Code anti-pattern did not significantly hurt comprehension, but their combination did ([Abbes et al. 2011](https://publications.polymtl.ca/17390/)), as did two occurrences of either ([Politowski et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.02438.pdf)). An explainer should flag where smells co-occur, not catalogue every smell.

## Watching builds confidence faster than competence

The most consistent finding across visualization research is that what the learner does matters more than what the screen shows. A meta-study of 24 algorithm-visualization experiments concluded that **how students use the visualization has a greater impact than what it depicts** ([Hundhausen, Douglas & Stasko 2002](https://www.academia.edu/15636659/A_Meta_Study_of_Algorithm_Visualization_Effectiveness)), and over 40% of those studies found no significant effect ([Saarland lecture summary](https://www.rw.cdl.uni-saarland.de/people/diehl/private/SoftVisVorles/slides/07_SV_EducationEvaluation.pdf)). The resulting engagement taxonomy runs from viewing through responding, changing, constructing and presenting ([Naps et al. 2002](https://fileserver.tk.informatik.tu-darmstadt.de/Publications/2006/pvwTrees.pdf)). A non-interactive video sits on the lowest rung. Passive live coding, a format close to an animated code walkthrough, was "not different enough from static examples to affect learning" ([SIGCSE 2025](https://sigcse2025.sigcse.org/details/sigcse-ts-2025-Papers/4/Live-But-Not-Active-Minimal-Effect-with-Passive-Live-Coding)).

Recent AI-assistance studies show how easily people mistake exposure for understanding. In Anthropic's randomized trial, 52 mostly junior engineers learned the Trio async library. Those using an AI assistant **scored 50% against 67% for hand-coders (Cohen's d = 0.738)**, with no significant speed gain, and the largest gap was on debugging. Learners who generated code and then asked follow-up questions scored 86%, and those who asked only conceptual questions scored 65%. Those who delegated scored 39%. These pattern groups held only two to seven people each, and the quiz was immediate ([Anthropic 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills)). In METR's trial with experienced open-source maintainers, AI **increased task time by 19%, while developers had forecast a 24% speed-up and afterwards believed they had been about 20% faster** ([METR 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089)). Among novices, 10 of 21 showed metacognitive difficulties, often ending in an unwarranted "illusion of competence" ([Prather et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17739)). A polished explainer video is a passive explanation by construction, so the same gap between feeling and knowing applies to it. "I get it now" is not evidence.

What a short video can embed is limited but real. Practice testing and distributed practice are the two "high utility" techniques. Self-explanation and interleaving are "moderate", and rereading and highlighting are "low" ([Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)). Secondary summaries of the testing-effect meta-analyses report g ≈ 0.50 to 0.61, with larger effects when the final test comes a day or more later ([summary of Rowland 2014 and Adesope et al. 2017](https://studycardsai.com/blog/active-recall-statistics)). Inside video, the results are weaker and less stable. Brief tests between segments of a 21-minute lecture reduced mind wandering and improved learning ([Szpunar, Khan & Schacter 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3631699/)), but a 2022 replication found only a small attention effect and no learning benefit ([Welhaf et al. 2022](https://www.pubmed.gov/35348931/)). Prequestions before each segment of a two-minute video improved retention in general ([Carpenter & Toftness 2017](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/heres-simple-way-boost-your-learning-videos-prequestion)), with limited effects in longer, authentic lectures ([Toftness et al. 2018](https://pure.royalholloway.ac.uk/en/publications/the-limited-effects-of-prequestions-on-learning-from-authentic-le/)). Self-explanation helped across 64 studies ([Bisra et al. 2018](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/self-explanation-powerful-learning-technique-according-meta-analysis-64-studies)). Yet adding self-explanation prompts on top of worked examples *weakened* the worked-example effect in mathematics ([Barbieri et al. 2023](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1.pdf)). The defensible design is therefore one or two prediction or retrieval beats per video, placed at segment boundaries and followed by the answer. A quiz every thirty seconds is not supported.

Architecture understanding is finished outside the video. Dagenais's teams presented an architecture overview in newcomers' first days, "but it was only through exploration, i.e., questions, technical meetings and experimentation, that newcomers finally understood" it. Learning material helped only when it addressed current tasks and was immediately applicable, and **half of participants said the most valuable thing was compiling, running and experimenting with the product** ([Dagenais et al. 2010](https://www.cs.mcgill.ca/~martin/papers/icse2010.pdf)). Video research agrees on its role. Across 105 randomized trials, **replacing teaching with video gave g = 0.28, while adding video to existing teaching gave g = 0.80** ([Noetel et al. 2021](https://www.aera.net/Newsroom/Video-Improves-Learning-in-Higher-Education-A-Systematic-Review)). An explainer works best as a map that sends viewers into the territory with a concrete task and a prediction to check, not as a substitute for the territory. Spacing, the other high-utility technique, cannot happen inside one video. It needs a series that opens each episode with a recap question about the last.

## Motion pays only when it shows change the eye must track

### Animate behaviour and transitions, not the text itself

Animation beats static graphics only modestly and conditionally. Across 61 experiments and 140 comparisons (N = 7,036), the average advantage was **g = 0.226**. It was smaller for abstract representations such as formal notation, symbols and diagrams than for iconic ones, and 10% of comparisons favoured static graphics ([Berney & Bétrancourt 2016](https://tecfa.unige.ch/perso/sandra/pdf/Earli2016_berney_betrancourt_FINAL.pdf)). Representational animation (d = 0.40) and procedural-motor content (d = 1.06) gained most ([Höffler & Leutner 2007](https://www.leibniz-ipn.de/en/research/publications/instructional-animation-versus-static-pictures-a-meta-analysis)). When information content was held equal, animation showed no reliable advantage for conveying complex systems. Tversky and colleagues traced the failures to two principles: graphics must be readily perceived (apprehension), and their structure must match the content (congruence). They named one exception: animation can help with reorientation in time and space ([Tversky, Morrison & Bétrancourt 2002](https://hci.stanford.edu/courses/cs448b/papers/Tversky_AnimationFacilitate_IJHCS02.pdf)). Code is formal notation, the category that gains least from animation. Animating what code *does* is congruent with the content: a request travelling, state changing, structure being reorganized. Typing code onto the screen is not.

Token morphing is a special case of animated transitions between graphic states, which do have direct evidence. In two controlled experiments, **animated transitions improved perception of change through object constancy**. Simple staging and stable landmarks helped, and morphing an object into an unrelated one misled viewers ([Heer & Robertson 2007](https://idl.uw.edu/papers/animated-transitions)). For comparison and analysis, though, animation lost. **Small multiples were more accurate and faster than animated trends** ([Robertson et al. 2008](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~stasko/papers/infovis08-anim.pdf)). The split is useful. Morph when the question is "how did this become that". Show two static panels side by side when the question is "which of these is better". No study has compared animated code morphs with static diffs for comprehension.

Video is system-paced: words disappear as they are spoken. That makes transience the medium's distinctive risk. The usual advantage of narration over on-screen text **reverses for long spoken passages**, because transient audio overloads working memory ([Leahy & Sweller 2011](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/acp.1787)). The remedies are shorter chunks and persistent visual anchors ([Ayres & Paas 2007](https://studio.courseware.epfl.ch/assets/courseware/v1/61858f819cd4b08668b0f41c1fabd34c/asset-v1:EPFL+DEMO+2020+type@asset+block/ZA.pdf)). Good design matters more for complex material and for system-paced media than for self-paced media ([Noetel et al. 2022](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/8zqy4/multimedia-design-for-learning-an-overview-of-reviews-with-meta-meta-analysis)). The table below summarizes the levers with the best independent support and the boundary conditions that matter for a code explainer.

| Segmenting into meaningful chunks | Small-to-medium; 56 studies, 88 comparisons ([Rey et al. 2019](https://is.tuebingen.mpg.de/publications/rey2018segmenting)) | Holds for system-paced video; higher-prior-knowledge learners gained more on retention |
| Cutting seductive details | Their inclusion costs g = −0.33; 58 studies ([ACU summary of Sundararajan & Adesope 2020](https://inspire.acu.edu.au/articles/seductive-details)) | Harm is larger when viewers cannot control pace, which is the case for video |

Mayer's own lab medians run higher: temporal contiguity d = 1.30, coherence 0.70, segmenting 0.70 ([Mayer 2017](https://synapsesocial.com/papers/W2625051696)). Those are best read as upper bounds from short, controlled, novice-learner studies. In a meta-analysis of 591 effects from Mayer's corpus, **removing seductive details was among the largest effects** ([NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10637927)). For a video tool, that means no music bed under explanation, no stock footage and no off-topic jokes.

Engagement data adds pacing guidance, with the caveat that it measures watch time, not learning. Across 6.9 million edX sessions on 862 videos, **median engagement capped at about six minutes regardless of video length**, and 75% of sessions on videos under three minutes lasted beyond three quarters of the video. Khan-style drawing tutorials drew **1.5 to 2 times the engagement of slides and code screencasts**, and faster, more enthusiastic speakers held attention, up to 254 words per minute when the visuals carried the content ([Guo, Kim & Rubin 2014](https://up.csail.mit.edu/other-pubs/las2014-pguo-engagement.pdf)). In a related analysis, 61% of interaction peaks coincided with visual transitions ([Kim et al. 2014](https://iis.seas.harvard.edu/papers/kim14video.pdf)). Machine narration is no longer a handicap. A modern TTS engine matched a human voice on perceptions, learning and cognitive efficiency ([Craig & Schroeder 2019](https://searchworks.jioinstitute.edu.in/articles/eric__EJ1225297)). With 447 participants, AI-generated videos produced no exam difference from human-made ones, were rated slightly worse for learning experience, and took hours rather than days to make ([Netland et al. 2025](https://x-mol.com/paper/1857628113137397760)). On-screen words should be labels, not transcripts. Exact correspondence between on-screen text and narration impairs learning, while brief labels help ([Yue, Bjork & Bjork 2013](https://sites.lifesci.ucla.edu/psych-bjorklab/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/YueBjorkBjork2013_redundancy.pdf)). Toggleable captions, which more than 100 studies link to better comprehension and memory, are a separate matter ([Gernsbacher 2015](https://successforkidswithhearingloss.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/Video-Captions-Benefit-Everyone_2015.pdf)).

### Boxes and arrows need discipline more than notation

Practitioners explain systems with informal sketches, not UML. **35 of 50 professional engineers used no UML at all** ([Petre 2013, via Never Work in Theory](https://neverworkintheory.org/2013/06/13/uml-in-practice-2.html)), and the whiteboard was the most common sketching medium among 394 surveyed developers ([Baltes & Diehl](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.09172)). Informal boxes and arrows are the right vocabulary, but they fail in predictable ways. C4 describes the typical result as "a confused mess of boxes and lines", with ambiguous elements, unlabelled relationships and mixed abstraction levels ([C4 model](https://c4model.com/introduction)). Its checklist asks for a title stating diagram type and scope, a legend, a type and short responsibility for every element, one-directional lines labelled with their intent, consistent colour, and attention to colour blindness ([C4 notation](https://c4model.com/diagrams/notation)). The empirical layout findings support part of that list. **Edge crossings were the most damaging of five graph aesthetics tested** ([Purchase 1997](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/3-540-63938-1_67.pdf)). Layout quality increasingly drives performance as UML diagrams grow, and novices benefit from good layout more than experts do ([Störrle](https://orbit.dtu.dk/en/publications/diagram-size-vs-layout-flaws-understanding-quality-factors-of-uml/)). Moody's Physics of Notations supplies useful heuristics such as semiotic clarity (one symbol per concept) and manageable complexity ([Moody](https://homepages.uc.edu/~niunn/courses/RE-refs%5CPoN-RE09.pdf)). A review of 70 applications, though, found most adopted it "with little critical thought" ([van der Linden & Hadar 2019](https://researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk/en/publications/a-systematic-literature-review-of-applications-of-the-physics-of-/)), so treat it as a checklist rather than as laws.

Two cautions keep the style honest. Minimalism is not a law either. Embellished charts were interpreted as accurately as plain ones and were **recalled better after more than 12 days** ([Bateman et al. 2010](https://vis.csail.mit.edu/classes/6.859/readings/pdfs/Bateman-UsefulJunk.pdf)), so a distinctive visual identity tied to meaning is fine. And impressiveness is not evidence. **62% of proposed software visualization approaches included no evaluation at all** ([Merino et al. 2018](https://scg.unibe.ch/research/softvis-eval)). The well-known 3D code-city result came from one author-run experiment against an Eclipse-plus-Excel baseline ([Wettel, Lanza & Robbes 2011](https://www.inf.usi.ch/faculty/lanza/PUBS/M/Wett2010a.pdf)). Plain 2D boxes, few per frame, laid out to avoid crossings on the path being narrated, rest on firmer ground.

## What unblackbox should encode in its spec and its agent

unblackbox's current design already matches the strongest levers, which is worth stating before listing gaps. Its JSON spec is a sequence of beats, each pairing one or two spoken sentences with the visual change they name. That is temporal contiguity and short-chunk narration, the fix for transience. The authoring guide caps narration at about 25 words per beat and code at about 14 lines. `focus` dims everything except the narrated lines or element, which is signaling. `morph` lets unchanged tokens glide to their new positions, which is object constancy. `clear` between sections segments the video and enforces coherence. Rejecting hex colours in favour of four semantic colours gives the consistent colour mapping C4 asks for, and the `.srt` sidecar gives toggleable captions without burned-in redundancy. The evidence puts the remaining leverage in three places: the *structure* of the beats, a few missing visual operations, and the *agent* that decides what to say.

The beat structure below is a synthesis of the evidence, not a tested template. It assumes one question per video, a total under about six minutes, and chapters of one to two minutes.

| Phase | What it does | Evidence | unblackbox today → needed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hook (≤30 s) | A concrete failing scenario, bug, or question about the code | 30-second motivation ([SoME1](https://www.3blue1brown.com/blog/some1)); problem-first g = 0.36 | Code and text elements suffice |
| Name the parts | At most about four role-named boxes, each defined in plain words | Four-chunk limit; pre-training; subgoal labels | `box` exists → lint that counts new names per scene |
| Place it | System context, then zoom into the one container that matters | C4 levels; animation helps reorientation | `move`/`clear` only → a camera zoom that keeps the parent boundary visible |
| Run one request | A marker travels along labelled arrows while the active path is lit | Control flow first; trace view 22% faster, 43% more correct; crossings on the traced path matter most | `focus` takes one id → multi-element focus plus a travel-along-arrow action |
| Show state and data | Who owns which data, where it lives, what changes | Systematic tracing predicts correct changes | Box and text labels suffice |
| Contrast | Without-pattern version, then `morph` to the pattern, then name it | Case comparison d = 0.50; pattern names as chunks | `morph` exists → static side-by-side compare layout |
| Why | The decision, the rejected alternative, the constraint | Rationale is the scarcest knowledge | Narration only → a rationale source field in the spec |
| Where it breaks | Cross-component interactions and co-occurring smells | As-needed reading fails; smells compound | `focus` with the `bad` colour |
| Predict, then reveal | A pause prompt with a visible countdown, then the answer | Responding beats viewing; prequestions | `hold` only → a question beat type |
| Hand-off | One exploration task in the real repository, plus a one-line takeaway | Understanding forms through exploration | `text` h2 exists |

Several of the needed operations are cheap and deterministic and belong in `unblackbox validate` rather than in prose guidance. An **audience field** (novice, intermediate, expert) should change pre-training depth, whether patterns are unpacked or merely named, and pacing, because expertise reversal makes one-size scaffolding wrong for someone. The validator can warn on unlabelled arrows (C4), on more than about four new named boxes per scene (Cowan), on any identifier spoken before it appears or is defined (pre-training), and on narration that repeats on-screen code verbatim (redundancy). It can also estimate words per minute. The notes infer a default of about 150 to 180 wpm, slower for dense code, from Guo's engagement data. That figure is an inference, not a measured optimum. The `still` command could flag arrow crossings along the path being narrated. The theme needs one fix the evidence makes concrete. Computed against WCAG's 4.5:1 minimum for normal text ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html)), the `muted` colour (#768390) reaches only **4.18:1 on the background and 3.88:1 on code panels**, and `bad` (#e5534b) reaches 4.37:1 and 4.06:1. Separately, `good` and `bad` are a green and red pair, the most common colour-vision confusion. Each should be paired with a label or shape cue, as C4 recommends.

The agent is the riskiest component, because it is the ultimate expert: it sees the whole codebase at once. Experts are systematically the worst at predicting what novices will find hard. In Hinds's study, debiasing by recalling one's own early learning **did not reliably remove the bias** ([Hinds 1999](https://publish.obsidian.md/joelchan-notes/discourse-graph/sources/%40hindsCurseExpertiseEffects1999)). A prompt like "explain this for beginners" is that same failed debiasing. Structural controls work better. The agent can emit an explicit assumed-knowledge list for the chosen audience, run a "novice critic" pass that flags undefined terms and skipped causal steps, and require that the script supports answering one transfer question it did not show ("what happens if the cache is cold?"). LLM explanations have been rated easier to understand than student-written ones, yet neither was always correct ([Leinonen et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03938)), so claims about behaviour should be checked against the code or a real trace. Rationale needs the strictest rule. Code does not contain its own reasons. The agent should mine ADRs, commit messages, pull-request descriptions and issues, or ask the user. Where no source exists, it should say the rationale is unknown rather than invent a plausible one. That last rule is an inference, not a finding: viewers cannot tell an invented "why" from a real one, and the rationale beat is the part they are most likely to remember and act on.

Finally, unblackbox should measure learning, not watch time. The six-minute guidance is an engagement correlation ([Guo et al. 2014](https://up.csail.mit.edu/other-pubs/las2014-pguo-engagement.pdf)), and lab effect sizes shrink in the field. The same spec that drives the video could generate a short prediction quiz to take a day or more later, which is when retrieval effects are larger. Those quizzes could settle the open questions this research cannot: staged token morph versus static diff, zoom versus hard cut, and video with versus without a predict beat. No published study has measured any of them for code explainers.

## Conclusion

The evidence moves the centre of gravity away from the clean visual style the tool started from. The CodeAesthetic look plausibly works because of its restraint, not its motion: few elements, one change at a time, the problem before the pattern, and a dim-everything-else focus. Those are coherence, signaling, segmenting and problem-first sequencing, all well supported. Token morphing, the signature effect, is the least-evidenced part of the style. It is defensible as an object-constancy transition, but it has never been tested against a static diff. Meanwhile the two ingredients that most separate a durable mental model from a pleasant summary are a runnable trace and the reasons behind the design, and neither is a rendering problem. A code-reading agent can produce the "what" of an architecture cheaply. Its value depends on whether it picks a representative scenario, keeps each view within four chunks for the declared audience, and either finds the real rationale or admits it is missing.

The success criterion should change accordingly. An unblackbox video has done its job when a viewer, a day later, can predict what the system does in a case the video never showed, and has opened the code to check. A video that leaves viewers feeling they understand without being able to predict reproduces the illusion of competence measured in the AI-assistance trials. The most valuable thing unblackbox could add after a validator is a built-in way to find out which of the two it is producing.
